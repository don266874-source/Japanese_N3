Haru no Nihongo - Ep1: 初めて警察のお世話になった話

Felix's Learner Note:

This episode of Haru no Nihongo is one of the most practically useful things you can put in your ears if you plan to drive in Japan. Haru opens with her usual cheer, does a quick round of housekeeping about Sunny Side Japanese (the conversation community she runs with other teachers) and her free transcripts, then drops the hook with almost no warning: 事故りました - there was an accident. Not her accident. Her French boyfriend's. What follows is a full, unglamorous walkthrough of exactly what happens when a foreign driver damages a rental car in Japan, told at natural speed with all the digressions left in, which is precisely why it's good listening practice for N2 and N1 learners.

The setup matters, and she lays it out clearly: her boyfriend passed his test in France and came to Japan straight after, so he hadn't driven in years and never converted to a Japanese license. He was driving on a French license plus a translation - legal enough, but France is left-hand drive and Japan is right-hand drive, drifting left is the predictable outcome. He even took ペーパードライバー講習 lessons first (that word, 'paper driver,' is one of those Japan-specific terms worth knowing: someone whose license exists mostly on paper). Twenty minutes from home, leaving a family-restaurant car park, he clipped the 縁石 - the curb - blew a tire, and damaged the body. Then came the real lesson, the one nobody tells tourists: three phone calls are mandatory. The rental company's accident line. The police. And the branch you rented from. Because it's a rental, the police have to come and inspect the scene - three officers arrived for one lonely curb.

What makes this episode worth shadowing rather than just reading is everything after the crash. There's the emotional vocabulary of screwing up (ガチ凹み, やったわ, ビビった, 汗かき始めて), the comedy of being ferried around in a police car like it's a taxi, and then genuine culture: how kind those officers turned out to be, chatting about his Japanese and driving him back to the mall afterwards. She closes with the money shot - 300,000 yen of damage that came to exactly 0 yen because they'd paid roughly 1,500 yen extra for full insurance - plus a warning that enforcement on foreign drivers is tightening as accidents rise. If you're planning a Japan road trip, this is the episode to listen to before you pick up the keys.

📚 Grammar Breakdown

1. 〜てしまう → 〜ちゃう／〜ちゃった（完了・後悔の口語形）
Meaning: ended up ~ing; went and did ~ (often with regret)
In Text: 乗り上げちゃって (rode right up onto it), 壊れちゃった (it broke — damn), 事故っちゃった場合 (if you end up having an accident), タイヤがパンクしちゃって (and now the tire's gone flat). The whole accident is narrated in this form.
Note: Textbook Japanese teaches you 〜てしまう as 'finish completely,' but that's only half of it. In speech the other half is regret: it marks something that happened and can't be taken back. That's why Japanese people virtually never say 「壊れました」 about their own mistake — 「壊れちゃった」 carries the 'and now I'm in trouble' that English gets from tone of voice alone. The contraction is worth drilling as sound rather than grammar: 〜てしまう → 〜ちゃう, 〜でしまう → 〜じゃう. Once you recognise 〜ちゃった you'll hear it maybe twenty times a conversation, and producing it is one of the fastest ways to stop sounding like a textbook. Note also what Haru does with it: she uses the casual form even when reporting to the rental company, because the casual form is what makes her sound genuinely apologetic rather than formally polite.
2. 〜っていう / 〜というのは（話題のラベリング）
Meaning: the thing called ~ / the ~ that I mean / it's that ~
In Text: 彼氏が事故ったよという話で (it's the story where my boyfriend crashed), ペーパードライバー講習っていうのが日本にはありまして (there's this thing in Japan called paper-driver lessons), レンタカーで事故ったら警察呼ぶんだっていうね (the thing where you call the police if you crash a rental),and when she defines the term outright: 長い間運転していなかった人のことをペーパードライバーって言うんですよ (we call someone who hasn't driven in ages a 'paper driver').
Note: This is the load-bearing beam of Japanese storytelling, and English has no tidy equivalent — which is why translations usually drop it and the result reads flatter than the original. Its job isn't quotation, it's labelling: Haru uses it constantly to slap a name on a situation so the listener has a file folder to put it in before she continues. When she says 「レンタカーで事故ったら警察呼ぶんだっていうね」, the っていうね at the end isn't quoting anyone — it's her handing you the takeaway with a shrug. Listen for it after unfamiliar words too: attaching 〜っていう lets a speaker introduce jargon (ペーパードライバー, 縁石, レッカー) and then explain it in the next breath, which is exactly what she does several times here. If you're writing or speaking and your paragraphs feel like a list of disconnected facts, this is usually the missing connector.
3. 〜みたいな（曖昧さ・にごし・共感の要請）
Meaning: sort of / like / that kind of thing
In Text: マジかよ、みたいな感じで (kind of a 'you've got to be kidding' feeling), 20万とか30万とかかかるみたいな (sounds like it'll run 200,000 or 300,000 yen), and 意見交換みたいな感じのディスカッション (a bit of an opinions-swap kind of discussion).
Note: Most learners file 〜みたいな under 'simile' and stop there, so it stays a decoration in their speech. Its real workload is vagueness as social lubricant. When Haru says 「30万とかかかるみたいな」, she's not comparing anything to anything — she's refusing to commit to a number she isn't sure about, and inviting you to share the feeling rather than check the figure. That's why it clumps with とか and なんか and 感じ: the cluster is how Japanese speakers signal 'don't hold me to the exact wording here.' English speakers tend to over-specify in exactly these spots, which can come across as oddly assertive. Notice too that it attaches to whole clauses, not just nouns — 「マジかよ、みたいな」 turns an entire sentence into a vague impression. Try using it once or twice per story you tell and see how much softer your Japanese gets.
4. 〜じゃん（共通認識の確認）と 〜かよ（突き放し）
Meaning: isn't it / come on, it's ~ (〜じゃん) — versus — you've got to be kidding / what is this, ~? (〜かよ)
In Text: 警察とか。ドキドキじゃん (the police, right? — heart-pounding), 目の前じゃん (it's right in front of you!),and then the 〜かよ side: マジかよ、みたいな (are you serious), タクシーかよ (is this a taxi or what?).
Note: These two sit on the same shelf and learners mix them up constantly, so it's worth learning them as a pair. 〜じゃん assumes you and I already agree, and recruits you as an ally — it's warm, and it's why 「目の前じゃん」 from a police officer lands as kindness rather than sarcasm. It also softens complaints: 「高いじゃん」 is much gentler than 「高い」 alone because it frames the speaker as 'surely you see it too.' 〜かよ is the opposite move: it throws the idea away. 「タクシーかよ」 is Haru laughing at the absurdity of being chauffeured by the police, and the target is a situation, never a person — point it at someone and it becomes a provocation instantly. Both are firmly casual; keep them for friends, inner monologue, and storytelling, and note that the shock value of 〜かよ makes it rare in polite company. Listen to this episode for the contrast: the police use the warm one, Haru uses the sarcastic one about herself.
5. 〜なきゃいけない（義務・手順）と 〜ておく（先回り）
Meaning: have to ~ / it's required that ~ — and — do ~ in advance (for later)
In Text: 警察に届けなきゃいけないんですよ (you have to file a report with the police), 警察に電話してくださいと (call the police), 現場検証をしなきゃいけない (they have to inspect the scene), 連絡しなきゃいけない (must contact) — and then 〜ておく: パスポートを持っておいた方がいいかも (might be wise to keep your passport on you), 覚えておいて (remember this / keep it in mind).
Note: 〜なきゃいけない is the casual reduction of 〜なければいけない, and practically speaking nobody says the full form in conversation — hearing it makes you sound like you're reading regulations aloud, which is ironic given that this episode is about regulations. Worth noticing is how Haru pairs it with 〜ておく, and that pairing is the actual advice of the episode: obligation is what happens after the accident, but preparation is what spares you. 「持っておいた方がいいかも」 (you'd probably better carry it) and 「覚えておいて」 (keep this filed away) are both 〜ておく doing its real job — acting now for the sake of a future you. English speakers over-use 'should' for both, and end up sounding vaguer than Japanese does; Japanese keeps 'must' and 'do it ahead of time' as visibly different shapes. That distinction is why this episode lands as genuinely useful rather than merely scary.
