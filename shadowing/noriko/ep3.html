Learn Japanese with Noriko (Intermediate) - Ep3: What's Your Everyday Pleasure?

Felix's Learner Note:

Episode 3 of Learn Japanese with Noriko opens with a warm bit of backstage talk: her interview with Miku-sensei of Miku Real Japanese in the previous episode. Noriko is genuinely generous about her peers here — she points out how many Japanese teachers now work as フリーランス (freelancers), each experimenting on Instagram, Twitter, YouTube or podcasts, and admits she respects them because she knows exactly how much work it takes. It's a nice reminder that the online Japanese-teaching world is less a competition than a loose, mutually supportive guild. Then she pivots to the real question of the day: 皆さんの毎日の楽しみは何ですか — what is your everyday pleasure?

Her own answer is refreshingly unglamorous: 朝のコーヒータイム (morning coffee time). She gets up, showers, and brews drip coffee from beans she buys carefully — and she describes those minutes as とても静かで穏やかな時間, a stretch with no irritation and no stress. That's the heart of it, and it's worth noticing how she phrases the value: it isn't a reward or an indulgence, it's the thing that gives her energy for everything else. She then reads a student's essay that lands on almost the same answer with a different drink — 朝の日本茶 (Japanese tea in the morning). The student wakes at 4:30, slips downstairs while the family sleeps, unloads the dishwasher, and then reaches the あこがれの時間 (the longed-for moment): tea made from 嬉野茶, a sweet, umami-rich green tea from Ureshino in Saga Prefecture, Kyushu, which she fell in love with during a homestay in Fukuoka eleven years earlier.

What makes this episode worth shadowing isn't the vocabulary list — it's that almost every sentence models how Japanese speakers actually talk about daily life, habits, and quiet satisfaction. You get a whole cluster of giving-and-receiving verbs (淹れてくれました, 説明してくれました, 連れて行ってくれて), the boiled-emotion grammar 〜て仕方がない (I can't help it), the perspective marker 〜にとって, the concession pattern 〜ても (even if), and the change-of-state pair 〜ようになる / 〜てくる. Noriko closes with the second-ever 今日のインスピレーション segment: a line attributed to Doraemon — にこにこしてるんだよ、いつも楽しそうに。そしたら本当に幸せになれる (keep smiling, always look like you're enjoying yourself — do that and you'll really become happy) — and connects it to the proverb 笑う門には福来る. Her advice if you're not feeling it: 最初は作り笑顔でもいいんですね — a fake smile is fine to start with.

📚 Grammar Breakdown

1. 〜てくれる（恩恵の受け手）
Meaning: Someone does ~ for me / does me the kindness of ~ing
In Text: ホストファミリーのお母さんはいつもおいしいお茶を淹れてくれました (my host mother always brewed lovely tea for me), お茶の店へ連れて行ってくれて (she took me to a tea shop), いろいろなお茶の種類を説明してくれました (she explained all the different teas to me), この生徒さんも私と同じようなことを書いてくれたんですが (this student wrote something very like my own answer).
Note: This is the grammar that carries most of the essay section, and there's a reason: Japanese makes you encode who benefits into the verb itself. 淹れた just says she brewed tea; 淹れてくれた says she brewed it for me, and quietly puts the speaker in a position of gratitude. Once you notice it, you start hearing it constantly — and once you use it, your Japanese stops sounding like a list of events and starts sounding like a person describing what others have done for them. Watch the politeness split too: くれた with family and friends, くださいました / くれました when the doer deserves distance. One trap English speakers fall into: because 〜てくれる already implies for me, adding 私に is usually redundant unless you need to contrast who got the favour.
2. 〜て仕方がない（抑えられない感情）
Meaning: Can't help ~ / unbearably ~ / ~ beyond measure
In Text: 大好きで大好きで仕方がありません (I love it, I love it, I simply can't help myself).
Note: Attach this to the て-form and you get an emotion you cannot contain — stronger than とても and far more physical. Note how the student stacks it: she doesn't just say 大好きです, she says 大好きで大好きで仕方がありません. That repetition is doing the work of "I love it, love it, love it," and English needs the triple to keep up. It's also a good window into how Japanese expresses intensity: often not with an adverb, but by letting the body lose control — 〜てたまらない and 〜てならない are its close cousins, and all three are things you say rather than write. Because it's a fixed phrase, it attaches freely: 心配で仕方がない (I can't stop worrying), 眠くて仕方がない (I'm unbearably sleepy).
3. 〜にとって（評価の立場）
Meaning: For ~ / to ~ / from ~'s point of view
In Text: 家族が皆寝てる間に、私にとって一人で自由に時間を過ごすのは重要なことです (while my family are all asleep, having free time alone is important to me), 私にとって嬉野茶のない人生が想像できません (for me, a life without Ureshino tea is unimaginable), and its casual twin 私には素晴らしいです (for me it's wonderful).
Note: 〜にとって doesn't mark the recipient of an action; it marks whose scale you're judging on. That distinction matters, because English to me blurs the two. 「私にとって早起きは大変です」 means "getting up early is hard in my case" — a judgement, not an event. Compare 私に (to me, direction) or 私のために (for my sake) and you'll see none of them fit here. It's a favourite of essay and interview speech precisely because it lets you own an opinion without claiming it's universal: 「誰にとっても」 shifts you straight back to everyone.
4. 〜ても（逆接の譲歩）
Meaning: Even if ~ / even though ~ / no matter how ~
In Text: 早起きは大変だと思う人がいますが (there are people who think getting up early is hard), 疲れている朝にお茶を飲んでから (even on a tired morning, after drinking tea), おきられなくて、早起きしにくい日もあります (there are days I can't get up, days when waking early is a struggle), 別に高い、いいお茶を買わなくてもいいと思うんですけれども (I don't think you necessarily need to buy expensive, high-grade tea), 嫌なことがあっても、悲しいことがあっても (even if something unpleasant happens, even if something sad happens).
Note: The essay is practically built on concession — the writer keeps granting the obvious objection before continuing, which is exactly what makes her writing persuasive: yes, 4:30 sounds brutal — but. Grammatically, take the て-form and add も (高くなくても, 飲まなくても). The repeated version is its own rhetorical device: 嫌なことがあっても、悲しいことがあっても stacks two concessions in a row, and Noriko's closing advice rides on that rhythm. Note also 〜にくい folded into one of these lines (早起きしにくい — hard to get up early), a compact way to say an action is difficult rather than listing tough.
5. 〜ようになる／〜てくる（変化・到達）
Meaning: Come to ~ / get to the point where ~ / gradually become ~
In Text: そうしたら本当に笑えるようになるんです (do that, and you'll genuinely come to be able to laugh), ママになってから、早起きに慣れてきて、朝型の人になりました (since becoming a mum I've got used to early mornings and become a morning person), 幸福になれます (you'll become happy).
Note: Where English tends to announce a change with a verb like became, Japanese often prefers describing the process: 〜なる states the destination, while 〜てくる frames it as something that has been developing up to now — so 慣れてきて isn't "I got used to it" but "I've gradually come to be used to it," which is precisely how habit formation actually feels. The pairing is common in reflective speech for good reason. Note the mechanism in Noriko's closing argument: she never claims smiling makes you happy instantly. She says a fake smile is fine, because the ability follows later — 笑えるようになる. That's the grammar doing philosophical work.
